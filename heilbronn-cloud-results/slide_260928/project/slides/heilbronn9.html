Certifying the 9-point Heilbronn triangle record

Abbreviations: T = the triangle x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 1 · VM = 4-core cloud virtual machine · UTC = Coordinated Universal Time

The problem

Place 9 points in the triangle T so that the smallest triangle any 3 of them form is as large as possible.

Best known set (Cantrell, 2006): smallest area 43/1568, shared by 13 triangles (one shaded). Proved optimal, to within 0.01%, only up to 8 points.

The method

1 · Reduce. An optimal set uses all 3 corners, or has at least 2 points on one side of T and at least 1 more on each other side.

2 · Model. Maximise the smallest area; one on/off variable per triangle for its orientation. Gurobi computes upper bounds (floating point).

3 · Split. Fix 5 orientations: 32 parts plus the corners case. A part is proved when its bound is ≤ 43/1568 × (1 + 10⁻⁴).

4 · Refine, check. Split open parts again (16 pieces). 7 and 8 points re-certified; a 7-point target just below the optimum is refused.

Problem. Heilbronn's triangle problem asks how to place n points in a region, here a triangle, so that the smallest triangle formed by any three of them is as large as possible. We use T = {x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 1}, of area 1/2. For 9 points the best known set is David Cantrell's (2006), on a 1/56 grid, with smallest area exactly 43/1568 (43/784 for a unit-area triangle); 13 of its triangles share that minimum. According to the project README, optimality is proved, to within 0.01%, only up to 8 points (n = 7 and n = 8 by Sudermann-Merx, arXiv:2607.15021, July 2026); the paper could not be opened from the cloud session. Goal. Show that no 9-point set beats 43/1568 × (1 + 1/10,000) = 0.0274262. Together with Cantrell's set this would show his set is optimal to within 0.01%, the same standard as the 8-point result. Method. (1) Every optimal set either uses all three corners, or has at least 2 points on one side and at least 1 further point on each of the other two sides. The project README attributes this to Proposition 1 of the preprint (not read here; not yet peer-reviewed). Our automated review sketched an elementary argument (two lemmas plus a computer enumeration), which a mathematician still has to check. (2) A mixed-integer model maximises the smallest area z, with one binary variable per triangle recording its orientation; Gurobi 13 computes an upper bound in floating point. (3) Fixing the orientation of triangles (2,3,k), k = 4..8, splits the problem into 32 parts plus the corners case; a part is proved when its bound is at most the target. (4) Unresolved parts are split on four more triangles (16 pieces) and re-run, repeatedly. Checks: 7 points and 8 points re-certified; a 7-point target just below the known optimum is correctly refused; results are combined with summarize.py plus a separately written coverage checker. The result rests on floating-point global optimisation with Gurobi's default tolerances (about 1e-6) against a safety margin of 2.7e-6, and on the reduction in step 1. On near-optimal 9-point sets the solver's value exceeded the exact value of its own points by up to 1.8e-6 (6.6e-6 on one degenerate set), so the four record-holding parts may need tighter tolerances to be provable.
